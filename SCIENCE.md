# Gut Intolerance Finder — Science

**What this is:** one card per food component: what it is, where it acts, the dose, the window, how strong the evidence is. The cards change when new research appears. Card numbers (3.1, 3.2…) are kept stable so references keep working.

**Values are our own**, set from published research (clinical studies, gastroenterology guidance and dietetic sources). The sources for each card are in `REFERENCES.md`. Where sources disagree, we pick a value within their range. Where no source gives a number, we estimate from how the component acts in the gut, and label it **Estimated**.

*Not medical advice. The product names suspects to test, never a diagnosis.*

### 3.1 Lactose

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Umbrella (level 1) | Enzymatic (low lactase) | — |
| Where it acts | Small intestine (draws in water), then colon | Measured |
| Main foods | Milk, yoghurt, ice cream, cream, and fresh cheeses such as cottage cheese and ricotta. Most other cheese is low, including brie and cheddar. | Reference table |
| Look-back window | Start: **30 min.** In lactose malabsorbers, undigested lactose reached the colon in ~50 min on average (give or take ~20 min) in people who got diarrhoea, ~75 min in people with pain and bloating, ~115 min in people with no symptoms. These times are after 50 g. At 12 g, transit took about five times longer. End: **8 h.** No study measures when lactose symptoms stop, so G3 applies: lactose acts like the polyols (it draws water into the small intestine, then ferments in the colon), so it gets their 8 h. Clinical tests stop watching at 3–4 h because of how the test is run, not because symptoms end; "30 min–2 h" is commonly quoted but unconfirmed; leaning wider (G5). In a blinded multi-day reintroduction trial, pain rose on day 3 of daily lactose doses (a day-level signal, not an hourly window). The NHS says symptoms can start within a few minutes. We keep 30 min because undigested lactose takes about that long to reach the colon, even in the fastest people. | Estimated |
| Dose threshold | e.g. **12 g within the window.** About 12–15 g (roughly one cup of milk) is well tolerated by most adults with presumed lactose intolerance (moderate-quality evidence, mostly studied in people who malabsorb lactose). EFSA agrees: the vast majority tolerate up to 12 g in a single dose with no or minor symptoms, though some people react below 6 g, so no single threshold suits everyone. Symptoms are very rare at ≤10 g in healthy people; no symptoms after 40–50 g rules intolerance out. | Measured |
| Stacking | Add all lactose within the window (G6) | — |
| Typical symptoms | Bloating, gas, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, rumbling, nausea, sometimes vomiting | Measured |
| How common | Triggered symptoms in 28% of IBS patients in a blinded trial, close to the 26% who reacted to a glucose placebo | Measured |
| Starting point | **2.8%** (trial 28% ÷ 10) | Estimated |
| Clinical test | Yes: hydrogen breath test, arranged through a GP, who may try cutting out lactose first → suggest seeing a GP | Measured |
| Caveat | A positive breath test shows malabsorption, not intolerance. Intolerance = malabsorption + symptoms. | Measured |
| Evidence strength | **Moderate** (several controlled dose studies with a consistent dose pattern; in the blinded IBS trial, close to the placebo rate) → normal suspect flag (G18) | — |
| Sources | R1, R2, R3, R4, R22, R23, R90, R91, R92, R93 (`REFERENCES.md`) | — |

### 3.2 Polyols: shared rules

Polyols (sugar alcohols) get **one card each**, because they differ in how much reaches the colon, in dose and in how often they trigger symptoms. These rules apply to all of them.

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Umbrella (level 1) | Fermentation / malabsorption (FODMAP "P") | — |
| Where they act | Only part is absorbed in the small intestine; how much varies by polyol and dose. The rest draws in water, then reaches the colon. | Measured |
| Look-back window | **30 min–8 h** for every polyol. No study reports when symptoms start. The start is set by how fast polyols travel: sorbitol took on average ~70 min (±~35 min) to reach the colon in people who got symptoms, and ~110 min in people who didn't, so a few arrive within about half an hour. A 1 h start would miss the faster third or so (G5). The end: breath tests show gas within 4 h, and in a multi-day trial, pain rose on day 1 (fastest of the FODMAPs). | Estimated |
| **Combined polyols** | Also add **all polyols together** within the window and flag above **20 g**. EU regulators consider polyols unlikely to cause laxative symptoms below 20 g a day from all sources. Sugar-free sweets often mix several (e.g. sorbitol + maltitol), so each can be under its own threshold while the total is over. | Measured (regulatory) |
| People with gut symptoms react at lower doses | In a blinded trial, 10 g of sorbitol or mannitol raised symptoms in IBS patients but not in healthy people. Thresholds below lean towards the low end of the evidence for that reason (G5). | Measured |
| Typical symptoms | Bloating, gas, rumbling, cramps, loose or watery stools; nausea at high doses | Measured |
| Clinical test | No standard test for individual polyols → guided experiment | Measured |
| Labels | Foods with more than 10% added polyols must carry "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects" (EU/UK). A useful hint for the food-matching step. | Measured |
| Sources | R5, R6, R7, R8, R9, R17 (`REFERENCES.md`) | — |

### 3.3 Sorbitol

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Main foods | Stone fruit, apples, pears, prunes, sugar-free sweets and gum | Reference table |
| Look-back window | 30 min–8 h (3.2) | Estimated |

### 3.9 Excess fructose

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Umbrella (level 1) | Fermentation / malabsorption (FODMAP "M") | — |
| Where it acts | Small intestine (draws in water) | Measured |
| Main foods | Apples, pears, mango, honey, agave, high-fructose syrups | Reference table |
| How it's calculated | Fructose in excess of glucose (fructose − glucose), not total fructose | Measured |
| Look-back window | e.g. **30 min–4 h.** Breath gas starts rising at ~55 min on average (fastest people ~25 min), so nothing is blamed in the first 30 min. Small-bowel water peaks ~75 min and is back to normal by ~3¼ h; breath gas starts rising ~45–55 min and peaks ~75–80 min. Symptoms "subsided after a few hours". | Estimated |
| Dose threshold | e.g. **6 g within the window, about one apple or pear.** No study has tested a dose of excess fructose in normal food, so this is the portion rule (G20). Pure fructose in water, in healthy adults: at 15 g, 0 of 20 malabsorbed it in one study and 1 of 10 in another; at 20 g, 4 of 10; at 25 g, from 10% to over half across 3 studies; at 50 g, 80% malabsorbed and 55% had symptoms. People vary widely, from under 5 g to over 50 g. Normal servings hold far less: about 6 g in a medium apple or pear, 8 g in a glass of apple juice, 14 g in pear juice, 1 g in a cup of grapes, under 1 g in orange juice. The closest test in real food: apple juice with about 9 g of excess fructose, where 7% of healthy adults malabsorbed it. One review reports symptoms from 14 g a day and low-fructose diets of under 6 g a day; we saw this in search results only. | Estimated |
| Limit of this method | Adding glucose lowered breath hydrogen but not symptoms in a blinded trial (26 patients, who also had more nausea). Glucose added to whole foods high in excess fructose changed neither (9 patients, 9 healthy people). So a low excess-fructose figure can't clear a food. | Measured |
| How common | Triggered 27% of IBS patients in a blinded trial, about the same as the 26% placebo rate. The trial dose was 60 g a day. | Measured |
| Starting point | **2.7%** (trial 27% ÷ 10) | Estimated |
| Stacking | Add all excess fructose within the window | — |
| Typical symptoms | Gas, bloating, abdominal pain, rumbling, loose stools, belching | Measured |
| Clinical test | Partly: breath test detects malabsorption, not symptoms → test, symptom link still needed | Measured |
| Evidence strength | **Moderate.** Consistent dose studies in healthy adults and a blinded IBS rechallenge with symptoms rising by dose, but close to the placebo rate in the largest blinded trial → normal suspect flag (G18) | — |
| Sources | R3, R39, R40, R41, R42, R43, R44, R45, R81, R82 (`REFERENCES.md`) | — |

### 3.10 Fructans

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Umbrella (level 1) | Fermentation (FODMAP "O") | — |
| Where it acts | Colon. Nobody digests fructans, so they all reach gut bacteria. | Measured |
| Main foods | Wheat, rye, barley, onion, garlic, leek, shallots, artichoke | Reference table |
| Amounts per food | **One working figure for every food tagged fructans: 2 g per 100 g**, the high end for common foods (onion 1.97). No open table covers everyday foods, and figures for one food disagree anyway: white bread is 0.14, 0.68 or 1.02 g per 100 g depending on the study. One figure needs no table of foods; it uses the tags and grams the tool already has. It overcounts low foods such as pasta (0.33). Limit: garlic is far higher but eaten in tiny amounts, so a clove won't reach the dose alone. | Estimated |
| Look-back window | e.g. **1–8 h.** Every measured time falls inside this. Breath gas peaked about 2 h after 10 g of fructans and about 4 h after chicory inulin. Colon gas was visible about 4 h after 40 g of inulin. The main IBS MRI trial measured symptoms over 5 h. In a meal diary, meals with 0.5 g or more of fructans were linked to fullness and bloating within 3 h. Nothing shows symptoms first starting after 8 h. The old 3–24 h came from fermentation carrying on, not from when symptoms start. | Estimated |
| Dose threshold | e.g. **100 g of fructan foods within the window (about 2 g of fructans), the portion rule (G20).** Nobody has stepped doses up in people with symptoms to find where they start; a dose-finding trial is registered. So every dose card with no tested threshold uses the same rule: about one normal portion, at or under the lowest amount tested. 2.1 g a day, on top of about 2.5 g already in the diet, raised symptoms more than gluten but not clearly more than placebo. Normal diets hold about 1–3.5 g a day. Healthy adults tolerated 5–10 g a day. 12 g a day worsened symptoms in people with quiet bowel disease; 20 g a day was the IBS trial dose. | Estimated |
| Stacking | Add all fructans within the window | — |
| Typical symptoms | Bloating, wind, abdominal pain, urgency | Measured |
| Normal-fermentation filter | Same rule as GOS (3.11): wind alone doesn't count. Inulin put more gas in the colon than fructose but caused few symptoms in healthy volunteers, and wind is the most reported effect at tolerated doses. | Measured (principle) |
| Coeliac rule | Wheat, rye and barley hold both fructans and gluten, so G19 applies here too: the coeliac message shows at the testing point (50%), and an experiment never removes them until coeliac disease is ruled out (3.16). Onion, garlic and leek can be tested first. | — |
| How common | **The most common FODMAP trigger:** 56% of IBS patients in a blinded trial, at 20 g a day, about 6 times or more what people normally eat. Pain rose on day 2 (slower than polyols). | Measured |
| Starting point | **5.6%** (trial 56% ÷ 10) | Estimated |
| Clinical test | No → guided experiment | Measured |
| Note | Some self-reported gluten sensitivity may be a fructan reaction (wheat contains both). In a blinded crossover of 59 people, symptoms scored 38.6 on fructans, 33.1 on gluten and 34.3 on placebo (scale 13–91). Fructans beat gluten, but not clearly placebo. | Measured |
| Evidence strength | **Moderate.** Strong that it's a common trigger (blinded trials), but the dose is unsettled → normal suspect flag (G18) | — |
| Sources | R3, R42, R45, R46, R47, R48, R49, R50, R51, R52, R53, R54, R85, R86, R87, R89 (`REFERENCES.md`) | — |

### 3.11 GOS (galacto-oligosaccharides)

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Umbrella (level 1) | Fermentation (FODMAP "O") | — |
| Where it acts | Colon. No human makes the enzyme to digest GOS. | Measured |
| Main foods | Beans, lentils, chickpeas, some nuts | Reference table |
| Amounts per food | **One working figure for every food tagged GOS: about 1.1 g per 100 g**, from haricot beans (0.96 g per 88 g). No open table covers everyday foods, and one figure needs no table of foods. | Estimated |
| Look-back window | e.g. **1–8 h.** Same reasoning as fructans (3.10), with less direct evidence. Breath gas peaked 4.5–6.5 h after a seed milk holding raffinose sugars. In a meal diary, GOS was linked to fullness and bloating within 3 h. | Estimated |
| Dose threshold | e.g. **90 g of GOS foods within the window (about 1 g of GOS, one serving of beans), the portion rule (G20).** Normal diets hold under 0.5 g a day. 2.8 g a day raised wind in healthy people at first. 6 g a day didn't raise symptoms in people with quiet bowel disease, though stools softened. 12 g a day of a manufactured GOS powder was the IBS trial dose. High-GOS diets set off symptoms in about 2 in 3 IBS patients, but the grams weren't published where we could read them. | Estimated |
| Stacking | Add all GOS within the window | — |
| Typical symptoms | Bloating, wind, abdominal pain, softer stools | Measured |
| How common | 35% of IBS patients in a blinded trial, at 12 g a day; pain rose on day 2 | Measured |
| Starting point | **3.5%** (trial 35% ÷ 10) | Estimated |
| Clinical test | No → guided experiment | Measured |
| Normal-fermentation filter | **Wind alone never counts as evidence.** Only logged pain, bloating or loose stools count. Wind after beans is common and usually fades: 45–50% reported more in the first week on pinto or baked beans (19% on black-eyed peas), against 3–11% on control diets, and at least 7 in 10 of those said it had eased by week 2–3. In one 12-week study, 15–23% still reported it in weeks 6–12. At 2.8 g of GOS a day, wind rose from 12 to 18 times a day, then fell to 11 after 3 weeks. | Measured (principle) |
| Evidence strength | **Moderate.** Blinded trial rate above placebo, and high-GOS diets set off symptoms, but no effect at 6 g a day and the dose is unsettled → normal suspect flag (G18) | — |
| Sources | R3, R45, R46, R47, R49, R53, R55, R56, R57, R86, R88 (`REFERENCES.md`) | — |

### 3.12 Coffee

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Umbrella (level 1) | Pharmacological (gut stimulant) | — |
| What we count | **Coffee, not caffeine.** Coffee stimulates the colon about as strongly as a large meal. Decaf had a smaller effect, and the studies disagree on whether it differs from regular coffee, so caffeine alone doesn't explain it. Decaf counts, and so do coffee and mocha soymilk. Coffee liqueurs don't. Caffeine from tea, energy drinks etc. is not counted here. | Measured |
| Where it acts | Colon (speeds up movement); also stomach acid and gallbladder | Measured |
| Look-back window | e.g. **0–2 h.** In people who say coffee affects them, colon activity rose within 4 minutes and lasted at least 30 minutes; a large diary study linked caffeinated coffee to diarrhoea 1–2 h later. Coffee acts within minutes, so its window starts at 0 (G2). | Measured (physiology) / association |
| Dose threshold | e.g. **25 g of any coffee drink, so any coffee counts, the portion rule (G20).** No study has found the lowest amount that sets off gut symptoms. The colon tests used 200 ml and 240 ml. Volume is a poor measure for coffee: espressos measured 23–70 ml with 51–322 mg of caffeine, while the 200 ml cup held about 75 mg. 25 g is about the smallest espresso. The 400 mg/day caffeine guidance is a safety limit, not a trigger dose. | Estimated |
| Stacking | Add cups within the window | — |
| Typical symptoms | Loose stools, cramps, pain, heartburn | Measured |
| How common | About 3 in 10 healthy people say coffee makes them need the loo. Among the triggers people with IBS report most often. | Measured |
| Starting point | **2.3%** (no trial; Moderate evidence). | Estimated |
| Normal-response filter | **An urge to go or a normal bowel movement never counts as evidence.** Only logged loose or watery stools, cramps, pain or heartburn count. 29% of 99 healthy young adults said coffee made them want to go, and colon activity rose in those people but not in the others. That is a normal response. | Measured (principle) |
| Clinical test | No → guided experiment | — |
| Evidence strength | Moderate for the effect on the colon; weak for symptoms and timing. The timing comes from diary data, and a month-long diary study of women with IBS found no link between same-day caffeine and symptoms. | — |
| Sources | R58, R59, R60, R61, R62, R97, R98 (`REFERENCES.md`) | — |

### 3.13 Alcohol (not built)

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Build status | **Not built, with no plan to add it**. The research stays here so it isn't lost. | — |
| Umbrella (level 1) | Irritant (raises gut permeability); Open whether to group it as Pharmacological | — |
| What we count | Grams of alcohol, not drinks, because a "drink" isn't one size: a UK unit is 8 g and a US standard drink is 14 g. **Rum, dessert and fortified wines also count toward excess fructose (3.9)**; mixers are counted as their own foods. | — |
| Look-back window | e.g. **4–36 h (mostly the next day).** In a month-long diary study of women with IBS, heavy drinking was linked to diarrhoea, pain and nausea the **next day** across the group. | Dose threshold | Open: research doesn't settle a number. The only dose evidence is that diary study: 4 or more drinks a day went with more next-day symptoms, and 1–3 drinks with little or no link. | Stacking | Add all alcohol in the same day | — |
| Typical symptoms | Diarrhoea, stomach pain, nausea, indigestion | Measured |
| How common | About 3 in 10 people with IBS report symptoms from wine or beer (survey) | Measured (self-report) |
| Starting point | **0.7%** (no trial) | Estimated |
| Clinical test | No → guided experiment | — |
| Evidence strength | **Weak**. One diary study, in women only, with no link within a person; the app study's link is an association. → soft flag (G18) | — |

### 3.14 Histamine (v2)

| Rule | Value | Measured or estimated |
|---|---|---|
| Build status | **Moved to v2**: niche and hard to work with. The research stays here. | — |
| Umbrella (level 1) | Pharmacological (amines) | — |
| **Message (shown to users)** | Histamine might be behind your symptoms, but there's no reliable test for it, and it can be confused with other conditions, such as a stomach ulcer or long-lasting hives. We recommend seeing a GP to rule these out. This is a precaution, not a sign that something is wrong. | Measured |
| Why (internal, never shown) | In a blinded trial, people who believed they had it could not reliably reproduce their own symptoms. A German-speaking allergy guideline says the blood test (DAO) has no diagnostic value. | Measured |

| Clinical test | No reliable test → rule out other causes first, then a food and symptom diary and a stepwise diet with a clinician. How long differs by source (4–8 weeks in one review; 10–14 days, then up to 6 weeks of testing foods, in the guideline). | Measured |
| Evidence strength | Weak | — |

| What we count | Grams of foods tagged wheat, rye or barley. The food tables give no gluten figure, so bread's is used for all: about 5 g per 100 g (Coeliac UK: 3 g in 60 g). | Estimated |

## 4. Not yet carded

**Carded so far:** the six FODMAPs from the blinded reintroduction trial (fructans 56%, mannitol 54%, GOS 35%, lactose 28%, fructose 27%, sorbitol 23%), the other common polyols, coffee, and wheat and gluten. Alcohol (not built), histamine (v2) and non-nutritive sweeteners (v2) keep their cards for the research.

**Leads (not enough evidence for a card yet):**
- **Dietary fat / fried food:** about half of IBS patients blame it; fat makes the colon's response slower to start and longer-lasting. No timing or dose data in people with symptoms.
- **Chilli (capsaicin):** often reported as a trigger; no timing or acute dose data.
- **MSG:** unverified, reactions within 2 h at 2.5–5 g, mainly without food.

Other amines, salicylates, sulfites, other additives, saponins/lectins. Cards to be added once dose, timing and symptom research is in.
